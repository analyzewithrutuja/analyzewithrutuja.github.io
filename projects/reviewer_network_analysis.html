Your Friends Shop Like You: Mapping 46,236 Yelp Reviewers

Does Yelp's "Elite" badge just reward good writing — or are Elite reviewers actually more socially connected too? This project reuses the same 46,236 clothing reviewers from the Fast Fashion vs. Slow Fashion analysis, but instead of asking what they wrote, it asks who they're friends with — turning Yelp's own social graph into a real network.

00 · The idea, first

Is Yelp secretly a social network?

Every Yelp user has a friends field — people they've connected with on the platform, the same way you'd add a friend on Facebook. Nobody analyzes this data for its own sake. But it raises a real question about the 46,236 clothing reviewers from the Fast Fashion project: are the ones Yelp calls "Elite" just better writers, or are they also sitting at the center of an actual social network — while everyone else is scattered around the edges?

This page answers that with real graph theory — nodes, edges, degree, clustering, homophily — applied to real people, not a toy dataset. Every concept is explained in plain language before it's used, so no prior graph-theory background is assumed.

01 · Intake

Building the friend graph

The friends field in Yelp's user.json lists every friend a user has across the entire platform — restaurant reviewers, nail salon reviewers, everyone. Most of those friends have nothing to do with clothing. The first job was narrowing that down to just the friendships that matter for this analysis: clothing reviewers who are friends with other clothing reviewers.

How the filtering actually worked

A script (extract_friends.py) streamed the 3.2GB user.json file line by line — never loading the whole file into memory — and kept an edge only when both people were already in the 46,236-reviewer set built for the Fast Fashion project.

Why this matters: this is the subgraph induced by the clothing-reviewer set — not the full Yelp friend graph, and not any single user's full friend list. A user might have 500 friends on Yelp overall, but only the handful who also reviewed a clothing business show up here. That's a deliberate scope choice — it isolates exactly the social structure relevant to this analysis.

02 · Reviewer categories

Not everyone gets treated the same

"Elite vs. regular" — the split used in the Fast Fashion project — hides a lot of variation. A reviewer with 1 review and a reviewer with 45 reviews are both technically "regular," but they're not comparable. Before touching the network, every one of the 46,236 reviewers was split into 6 categories using thresholds pulled from the actual data, not picked arbitrarily.

Where the cutoffs came from

Yelp's elite field lists which years a user held Elite status (e.g. "2019,2021,2022"). Counting those years gives an elite reviewer's tenure. For everyone else, review_count measures activity level.

The "Single-review" cutoff (review_count ≤ 1) isn't new — it reuses the exact review_credibility definition from the Fast Fashion project, where single-review accounts were already shown to rate more extremely (39.1% give 1-star vs. 18.7% for experienced reviewers).

The actual decision logic

This is exactly what categorize() in network_analysis.py does for every one of the 46,236 reviewers — elite status is checked first and always wins, so a reviewer's category depends on which branch they fall into, not just one number.

Why the order matters: is_elite is checked before any review_count logic runs — so a reviewer with 500 reviews who happens to also be elite is still classified purely by elite tenure, never as "Power User." Elite status always takes priority over raw activity volume.

03 · Network topology

Is this even a real network?

Before any findings, the basic vocabulary: a node is a reviewer, an edge is a friendship between two reviewers, and a reviewer's degree is simply how many friends they have inside this specific 46,236-person set. A component is a cluster where everyone can reach everyone else through some chain of friendships. The clustering coefficient measures something more specific: if A and B are both friends with C, are A and B also friends with each other? Real social networks say yes far more often than random chance would.

05 · Do friends shop alike?

Homophily: birds of a feather

Homophily is the tendency for people to be friends with others who are similar to them — the "birds of a feather" effect studied throughout social network research. Here, it's tested directly: do friend pairs share the same dominant fashion_type more often than random chance would predict?

Friends share a dominant fashion_type 9.1 percentage points more often than chance — real homophily, not coincidence. But averaging across all three types hides the sharper story on the left.

The aggregate number hides a sharper pattern. Mixed Retail–Mixed Retail pairs are the most over-represented combination of any (~3× expected) — department-store reviewers cluster tightly with each other. Fast Fashion–Slow Fashion cross-pairs are the most under-represented (observed less than half of what chance predicts) — casual mass-market reviewers and bespoke/bridal reviewers largely avoid each other's friend circles. Slow Fashion–Slow Fashion pairing is also below chance, unlike the other two same-type combinations — suggesting Slow Fashion friendships form for reasons other than shared bespoke/bridal taste specifically (geography, life stage, etc.).

07 · Does being social make you happier?

More friends, nicer reviews — up to a point

Binning reviewers by degree and averaging their star ratings and VADER sentiment (both from the Fast Fashion project) tests one more angle: does social connectedness itself correlate with how positively someone reviews?

Avg. VADER sentiment by degree

Both ratings and sentiment rise with degree, then plateau around 6+ friends. More socially connected reviewers do rate and write somewhat more positively — but the effect saturates rather than growing without bound, which is itself informative: there's no runaway "echo chamber" amplification here, just a modest, bounded social-happiness effect.

08 · So what

What this means, beyond the graph

Every finding above, turned into an actual takeaway — for this dataset and for the bigger picture.

What this network actually shows

1. Most reviewers are alone, but there's a real tight-knit core. 61.1% have zero clothing-reviewer friends — yet the 37% who aren't form one giant, genuinely clustered component (clustering coefficient 0.26), not a random scatter.

2. Elite status is a network signal, not just a writing-quality one. Veteran Elite reviewers are ~44× more connected than Single-review accounts, in a clean, exception-free gradient across all 6 categories.

3. Friends share taste — unevenly. Mixed Retail reviewers cluster ~3× tighter than chance; Fast and Slow Fashion crowds actively avoid each other's friend circles (well under half of chance-level overlap).

4. Geography beats shopping taste as a predictor of friendship. 70.5% of friend pairs share a metro (vs. 14.7% expected) — but 29.5% are cross-metro, a real long-distance signal, not noise.

5. Being social nudges ratings up, but only up to a point. Stars and sentiment both rise with degree, then plateau past ~6 friends — a bounded effect, not a runaway echo chamber.

Rating-volatility risk for Slow Fashion

Single-review accounts (Section 02) skew toward extreme ratings — a fact already known from the Fast Fashion project. Section 04 now shows those same accounts disproportionately review Slow Fashion businesses (43.8% vs. 12.6% for Veteran Elite). Slow Fashion's online reputation is therefore more exposed to the least predictable raters than Fast Fashion's is — a concrete, previously undocumented risk.

Elite status is a network signal, not just a quality one

If a platform treats "Elite" purely as a content-quality label, it's missing half the picture. Section 04 shows Elite status also predicts network centrality (44× more connections) — an Elite review isn't just more trustworthy, it also reaches further through the reviewer community.

Marketing & referral targeting

Mixed Retail reviewers cluster unusually tightly (Section 05) — referral/word-of-mouth campaigns should work well there. Fast and Slow Fashion crowds barely overlap, so cross-selling between them via social channels will be an uphill fight. Geographically, New Orleans and Tampa (Section 06) are the most socially fertile metros for word-of-mouth; Boise is the least.

Where this technique generalizes

The same graph-theory toolkit here — degree, components, clustering, homophily testing — is exactly what platforms use for fake-review/fraud detection (isolated, coordinated clusters are a red flag), influencer identification (high-degree nodes), and recommendation systems (homophily is the core idea behind "your friends also liked X").

09 · Honesty check

Limitations & honest assessment

Stated plainly rather than glossed over.

LimitationWhat it meansFlag
Metro assignment is approximate k-means clustering, not exact city ground truth — a small number of businesses near cluster boundaries could be mislabeled medium
No formal significance testing homophily is measured as observed-vs-expected, not validated with a p-value or permutation test medium
Sample network is a slice the 90-node visual (Section 03) is a BFS sample from one hub, not the full 46,236-node graph labeled as such
Friend graph is a snapshot reflects friendships at the time the Yelp dataset was collected — real relationships change over time medium
"Dominant" labels simplify mixed behavior a user's single most-frequent fashion_type/metro stands in for potentially varied real behavior medium
Descriptive, not causal every finding is a correlation/pattern in the network — no controlled experiment establishes why medium

Bottom line: this is a genuine, defensible graph-theoretic read of a real social network — not a claim that social connectivity causes better ratings or that Yelp's Elite algorithm explicitly rewards network position. The strongest result here is the 44× category gradient (Section 04): it's monotonic across all 6 categories with no reversals, and it's corroborated by an independent line of evidence from the original Fast Fashion project (elite reviewers already rate more positively) rather than a repeat of the same measurement.
